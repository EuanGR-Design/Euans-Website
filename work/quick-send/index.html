How I work

The fine details, and the big picture.

On their own, the small details of a UI hardly register: how a menu opens, where a field lands, how a countdown feels. Added together, they're why a product feels refined, even when you can't say why. You know it when you use it.

The problem

Sending a document was harder than it should be.

Most trials ended before a first send, the moment a trial succeeds. I grouped in-app feedback into themes, interviewed and tested with real users, and watched session recordings of people doubling back, hitting dead ends and rage-clicking. Nearly every clip was in the send flow, and nearly every one was a trial or new user.

What was wrong

The fix

  • Template first, send second

    To send anything, you first had to make a template. Clicking Send led to an empty template picker.

    The fix: Send straight from an upload

  • No clear start or end

    Sending was four steps in four places: upload, find it, make a template, then send.

    The fix: One flow, with one clear next step

  • Information overload, which caused decision paralysis

    Two sidebars showed every recipient, field and control at the same time.

    The fix: Simplify every screen

  • Power features for everyone

    Everything power users needed sat in front of people who just wanted to send.

    The fix: Reveal features when and where they're needed

The brief

Get a new user from sign‑up to a sent document in their first session.

Key decisions

The decisions that shaped it.

Recipients before fields

Add people first, and it's obvious who each field is for.

One sidebar

More room for the document, and narrow enough to embed.

Three fields by default

Signature, text and date. Admins can change the set.

A panel that follows you

Select a field and the sidebar becomes its editor.

Modals for occasional tasks

Recipients and settings stay out of the way until needed.

A Send you can't hit by accident

Bold, explains what's missing, and counts down before sending.

Design and product

Bringing design into product decisions.

As the designer and the product owner, I could see across the backlog, the roadmap and the build at once, and connect dots others couldn't. That made the work quicker and cheaper, and what shipped matched what we intended.

  • Opportunities in the backlog

    I worked through the backlog of tickets to find where one piece of work could solve several problems.

  • Scope and the brief

    I defined what Quick Send would and wouldn't do, and wrote the brief.

  • The right order

    I knew what was coming on the roadmap, so I sequenced the work: build Compose, test it through Quick Send, then take it to the editors.

  • Design and engineering aligned

    Regular sessions with the developers settled dependencies and trade-offs early, so we stayed on the same page throughout.

The result

Upload, add recipients, place fields, send.

Quick Send in production, start to finish, with no template in sight.

The outcome

What changed.

  1. First sends stopped being a problem.

    Trial users went from struggling to reach a first send to sending without trouble. Quick Send was the main driver.

  2. Tested live, without the risk.

    Quick Send launched as a beta, so the new editor got real feedback without touching anyone's everyday sending.

  3. It also led to Compose.

    A by-product, not the brief: the same editor now powers Compose, which customers embed in their own products, and the redesigned main editor.
